# MM01: Price-time priority order book

## Question and acceptance criteria

Can a minimal single-instrument limit order book enforce best-price then FIFO matching, handle crossing orders and partial fills, and conserve cash and quantity? This milestone implements and tests those behaviors. It preserves the existing `Account`, `simulate`, `experiment`, and CLI interfaces.

## Method

`lab.py` adds `LimitOrderBook`, immutable `LimitOrder` snapshots, and immutable `Trade` records. Each price has a deque; the best opposite price is selected with min/max over occupied price levels. Sequential submission IDs define acceptance time. A crossing order consumes resting liquidity at the resting price, stopping at its quantity or limit price. A partially filled maker retains its queue position; an incoming remainder joins the queue at its own limit. Completed orders and empty price levels are removed. Snapshot properties expose resting orders and historical trades without permitting mutation of the matching queues.

The matching convention follows the exchange explanations in [Coinbase Derivatives Order Matching](https://help.coinbase.com/en/derivatives/features/order-matching) and [Coinbase Order Execution Policy, section 7.1](https://www.coinbase.com/legal/eea/order-execution-policy), verified by opening the primary sources on 2026-10-19. This is a limited model of displayed continuous matching, not full exchange conformance.

Prices are positive Python integer ticks and quantities positive integer units. Each tick has unit cash value. Accounts begin at zero, permit negative cash/inventory, and use zero fees for book trades. Integer initial balances and integer operations preserve exact cash accounting even beyond floating-point integer precision. Self-trades are deliberately allowed and have zero net balance effect. Account objects remain mutable and should be treated as output by callers. Legacy `Account.fill` defaults and the quote-arrival simulator remain unchanged, including their floating-point accounting and 0.001 fee per filled unit.

## Reproduction and checks

From the repository, using installed Python 3.12.3 and NumPy 2.2.4:

```text
python -m unittest discover -s tests -v
python lab.py --output results/latest.json
```

The actual test run passed all 15 tests in 0.617 seconds (four existing tests and eleven new tests). Runtime is contextual evidence, not a matching-throughput benchmark. New tests cover empty/noncrossing books, price before time, FIFO on both sides, partial maker priority, incoming remainders becoming makers, exact limit boundaries, resting execution prices, level cleanup, invalid-input atomicity, immutable snapshots, large integer cash, and the self-trade convention.

A separate list-based oracle processes 1,500 seeded synthetic orders (Python random seed 641). It independently sorts eligible resting orders rather than using price-level deques. After every submission, tests compare the complete new trade sequence, every resting order, and each owner's expected cash/inventory. They also check every order's original quantity equals executed plus remaining quantity, aggregate submitted quantity equals twice traded quantity plus resting quantity, and any two-sided book is uncrossed.

## Actual findings

The regenerated [results artifact](../results/latest.json) contains a six-order example. A buy limit of 103 consumes maker IDs 2, 3, 1 in that order: 4 units at 101, 5 at 101, then 1 at 102. The older offer at 102 correctly waits behind the better-priced offers; equal-price offers execute FIFO. A later sell limit of 99 executes 4 units against a resting bid at 100. The final resting quantities are 2 bid at 100 and 2 offered at 102. Total traded quantity is 14; submitted quantity is 32, satisfying `32 = 2 * 14 + 4`. Cash and inventory sum to zero across accounts.

The seeded NumPy stress run submits 1,000 orders across eight owners, with independent uniform buy/sell sides, integer prices 95 through 105, and quantities 1 through 9. Actual recorded results:

| Metric | Value |
| --- | ---: |
| Submitted quantity | 4,881 |
| Trade records | 719 |
| Traded quantity (one side counted) | 1,956 |
| Traded notional in tick-value units | 194,390 |
| Resting orders / quantity | 192 / 969 |
| Aggregate net cash / net inventory | 0 / 0 |
| Events violating checked invariants | 0 |

The run checks aggregate cash, inventory, quantity accounting and uncrossed quotes after every event; `4,881 = 2 * 1,956 + 969`. Stronger per-order and owner accounting checks live in the independent-oracle test. Synthetic correctness checks establish no real-market performance or profitability.

The existing policy findings are preserved: symmetric terminal equity is 14.27932194331811 versus 13.22972829093095 with inventory skew. Skew reduces maximum absolute inventory from 13 to 5 and maximum drawdown from 4.107814212307062 to 0.578635338432278, but has lower terminal equity in this one paired seed. This negative equity comparison is retained; those metrics still come from the separate quote-arrival model.

## Limitations and interview preparation

All orders and market paths are synthetic. There is no exchange feed, calibration, cancellation, amendment, expiry, market-order API, hidden liquidity, auction, self-trade prevention, latency, credit validation, borrow constraint, or realistic fee schedule in the book. Borrowing, funding and transaction costs are zero in its scenarios. The baseline marks inventory at mid without liquidation costs. The book is not connected to the baseline market-making policy. Results are correctness evidence for specified cases, not an empirical market study or proof for every possible input. No novel algorithm, speed advantage, or quantum advantage is claimed.

The author should be able to trace the six-order example by hand; explain why price precedes arrival time and why a maker partial fill retains priority; distinguish a limit from the execution price; and distinguish a taker remainder from a maker partial fill. Explain both conservation identities: each trade transfers equal cash and opposite inventory, while one traded unit consumes two submitted units. An account's fill count counts execution records, not units. Zero-sum balances do not mean all submitted orders execute or that a strategy is profitable. Finally, explain why selecting min/max costs O(L) for L occupied price levels at each match iteration, while deque head operations are O(1), and why this simple implementation makes no production latency claim.
